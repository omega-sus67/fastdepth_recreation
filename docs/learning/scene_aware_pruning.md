# Scene-aware pruning experiment

**Status: experimental proposal under test, not a demonstrated improvement or verified research novelty.** The goal is to see whether considering how much a channel helps the most affected training scene can preserve depth accuracy across different rooms. It builds on our existing individual-channel ablation and physical pruning rather than inventing those ideas. The [fixed experiment configuration](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_scene_aware.json) records the design before scoring and training; consult the [progress report](../progress_report.md) for actual completed runs and results.

## Why consider individual scenes?

An ordinary average loss can hide a large error in a small subset of images. For example, removing a channel may barely affect bedrooms while hurting one kitchen substantially. We hypothesize that a channel important to a particular room should be less likely to be removed, even if its average effect is small. This is a testable idea, not proof that individual scene protection improves unseen scenes.

Our calibration sample uses **32 named training scenes, four images each**, selected deterministically from eligible scene names using seed 42. Two names, `furniture_store_0002a` and `furniture_store_0002d`, share one inferred location group, so these are **31 inferred groups**, not necessarily 32 different physical locations. Images are spread through each selected scene's available samples. The sample is balanced by named scene but still small and comes from bounded archive windows; it may not represent the complete dataset. The 445 tuning images are not used to compute scores. The original community checkpoint's training images are unknown and could include some of our NYU-derived samples, so neither training nor tuning independence from pretraining is established.

## The scoring rule

For one channel `j` and calibration scene `s`, define `L_s` as the average of the four per-image masked depth MAEs, and `L_s^(-j)` as the same loss after individually suppressing channel `j`. The effect is:

```
delta[s,j] = L_s^(-j) - L_s
average_score[j] = mean_s(delta[s,j])
scene_aware_score[j] = mean_s(delta[s,j]) + 0.5 * max_s(delta[s,j])
```

We remove the eight channels with the **smallest** score, breaking ties by channel index. The first rule is our matched average-ablation baseline; the second adds a penalty for the largest scene-specific loss increase. The coefficient 0.5 was fixed before the full run and is a design choice, not a proven optimum. If all scene deltas are negative, the maximum can also be negative; we do not clip it. The maximum uses **loss change**, not the scene's absolute loss: a difficult scene is not automatically treated as most affected by a particular channel. Noise in only four frames can make the maximum unstable, and single-channel effects can interact when eight channels are removed together.

`scripts/score_scene_aware.py` verifies the manifest and exact-overlap report, saves chosen paths and SHA-256 hashes, and runs both scores on the same 128 images. It temporarily masks one channel **after the second decoder's depthwise block and its normalization**, immediately before the pointwise layer. An earlier mask can be undone by BN offsets. The separate `scripts/build_scene_candidates.py` uses the resulting rankings to physically remove channels and update coupled filters, BN entries, depthwise groups and pointwise inputs, keeping the next 256-channel skip output unchanged. A candidate is not merely a zero mask.

## Fixed comparisons

We keep **192 of 200 channels**, so all four pruning methods have the same structure and parameter count:

1. **Random:** select eight channels randomly, with independent masks for seeds 42, 43 and 44.
2. **Weight magnitude:** remove channels with the smallest downstream pointwise weight L1 sums.
3. **Average ablation:** rank the mean loss change across the same 32 calibration scenes.
4. **Scene-aware ablation:** rank the mean plus half the largest scene-specific loss change.

The original model is included before and after matched recovery. For each seed, use the same 1,857 training images and shuffled order for the original and every candidate, one epoch, batch size 4, Adam 1e-5 and BN evaluation mode; affine BN parameters remain trainable. The objective is the **mean of per-image MAE** on finite positive depth targets; this differs from pooled valid-pixel MAE if images have different valid-pixel counts. Batch 4 changes optimizer step count and batch weighting relative to earlier batch-1 experiments; comparisons are within this protocol. The last batch contains one image, and each batch yields one optimizer step. `scripts/train_scene_batch.py` saves the initial/output hashes, shuffled-order hash, state, optimizer and random generator state. It does not yet provide a resume operation, so do not claim tested mid-epoch resume.

For the 445 tuning images, compare pooled RMSE, mean-image RMSE, δ1, each named scene's mean-image RMSE, and worst-scene behavior. Report every seed rather than only the best seed. Frames within scenes are correlated, scene labels are heuristic, and the tuning set has previously been inspected; statistical confidence and absolute worst-scene values need cautious interpretation. The 654-file evaluation collection is not used for training, scoring, or tuning selection. Desktop TensorRT FP16 tests, if completed, remain hardware-specific to the desktop GPU and use matching accuracy checks; Orin is needed before any target-hardware claim.

## How to explain it under questioning

- **What is the new idea?** In this project, combine a channel's average task-loss impact with the worst impact among sampled training scenes, then compare it with existing selection rules. Similar group-robust compression ideas have appeared elsewhere, so this is a candidate course-project contribution, not a claim to have invented the general idea.
- **Why use the maximum?** It makes the score sensitive to a scene that would otherwise be diluted in an average. The trade-off is that an outlier or noisy small scene can dominate.
- **Why keep width and training budget equal?** This holds the architecture size and recovery effort constant so the comparison focuses on which channels are removed. It cannot eliminate all sampling and training variation.
- **Why include random?** It gives a neutral reference; random channel pruning has been studied as a serious comparison, as in [Li et al., CVPR 2022](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2022/html/Li_Revisiting_Random_Channel_Pruning_for_CVPR_2022_paper.html).
- **Is worst-group pruning an established area?** Yes. For example, [group-robust pruning research](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02940) already studies group-sensitive compression in a different model domain. This focused search does not prove that our exact score and application are novel; assess the literature and the professor's criteria before any novelty claim.
- **Is it hardware-aware?** Not yet. This score uses training loss and scene identity, not measured Orin latency or energy. It can become part of a hardware-aware workflow only when candidate selection incorporates or is validated against target-device measurements.

## Actual tuning results — 14 November

All 15 recovery runs completed: five models (original plus four pruning methods) for each seed. The [run audit](../../results/training/2026-11-14-scene-aware-run-audit.json) checks the source and result hashes, manifest, settings and identical shuffled sample order within each seed. One implementation caveat: during the sweep the loss calculation was moved into a function for unit testing; it uses the same intended masking and arithmetic, but the full pre-refactor trainer source and exact trajectory parity were not saved. The reconstructed [refactor note](../../results/history/2026-11-14-scene-aware/trainer-refactor-note.txt) identifies which processes had already loaded the earlier version. The script saves state but has no resume operation.

On the 445-image tuning group, before extra recovery the original model had mean per-image RMSE **0.300091**, average-ablation pruning **0.290574**, and scene-aware pruning **0.284237**. The scene-aware candidate has 1,354,410 parameters, 6,376 fewer than the already-pruned community baseline's 1,360,786. All values are in native mirror depth units. These before-recovery checkpoints share one calibration sample; they are not independent repeated masks.

After one epoch on the new training data, the three-seed means of mean-image RMSE were:

| Method | Seed 42 | Seed 43 | Seed 44 | Mean of seeds |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Original, matched fine-tuning | 0.281928 | 0.286014 | 0.283210 | 0.283717 |
| Weight-based pruning | 0.284867 | 0.286782 | 0.282869 | 0.284839 |
| Average-ablation pruning | 0.282455 | 0.286012 | 0.282620 | 0.283696 |
| Scene-aware pruning | 0.281534 | 0.282815 | 0.282394 | **0.282248** |
| Random pruning | 0.284614 | 0.286970 | 0.285680 | 0.285755 |

The scene-aware result was 0.001448 lower than average-ablation in the average of the three seed-level mean-image RMSE values, and 0.001469 lower than the matched trained original. These small differences are observed on this tuning set; they are not proof of generalization, statistical significance, novelty, or target-device improvement. The random channel mask varies with the training seed, so random mask and training-order effects are not separately estimated. One training epoch and one lambda value do not establish optimal settings.

A post-hoc replay makes that caution particularly important. Repeating the original model's seed-43 run after the loss refactor used the same recorded order but produced 114 different model-state tensors, with maximum tensor difference 0.000608. Its tuning RMSE was 0.284011 rather than 0.286014: a **0.002003** change, larger than the 0.001448 three-seed mean difference between scene-aware and average-ablation methods. The cause is not isolated; the refactor, nondeterministic GPU operations and environment/runtime variation are possibilities. See the [state comparison](../../results/training/2026-11-14-scene-aware-refactor-replay.json) and [post-hoc tuning diagnostic](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-refactor-replay-tuning/summary.json). Therefore treat the apparent small advantage as an exploratory observation, not a reliable demonstrated gain.

A second same-code replay was then run with the same seed and recorded order. Compared with the first replay, it also had **114 different state tensors**, with maximum difference 0.000816 ([record](../../results/training/2026-11-14-scene-aware-same-code-replay.json)). Thus the refactor alone cannot explain all observed variation, although these checks do not identify the cause. The training script did not request deterministic algorithms for the original 15-run comparison.

In a separate diagnostic, we set `CUBLAS_WORKSPACE_CONFIG=:4096:8` before starting Python, requested `torch.use_deterministic_algorithms(True)`, disabled cuDNN benchmarking and set cuDNN deterministic mode. Two independent one-epoch runs of the original model with seed 43 produced **identical checkpoint file SHA-256 hashes and model-state tensors** in this desktop environment ([record](../../results/training/2026-11-14-scene-aware-deterministic-replay.json)). This is evidence for that model, seed and runtime, not a cross-platform guarantee or proof that the earlier comparison was reproducible. A [fixed follow-up protocol](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_deterministic_sweep.json) repeats all methods with deterministic mode; see the progress report for actual completion and results. The second run's identical hash includes the saved checkpoint file, but optimizer and RNG state semantics across other versions/devices remain untested.

[PyTorch's deterministic-algorithms documentation](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.use_deterministic_algorithms.html) describes this setting as requesting deterministic implementations where available, or raising an error for unsupported operations; the setting alone does not guarantee every aspect of application reproducibility. The [PyTorch 2.9 reproducibility guide](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/2.9/notes/randomness.html) also notes that results are not guaranteed across releases or platforms. Our byte-identical outcome is an observation for the recorded environment.

For a location-level view, scene variants with the same inferred room key are combined. The scene-aware model had a lower mean-image RMSE than average ablation in 15/24, 19/24 and 10/24 inferred location groups for seeds 42, 43 and 44 respectively. Worst-group RMSE was 0.630465, 0.658925 and 0.649765 for scene-aware, versus 0.627770, 0.659013 and 0.665786 for average ablation: the worst group is **not uniformly improved**. Group sizes range from one to 24 images, so a worst-group statistic can be highly unstable. [Per-model/per-scene metrics and checkpoint hashes](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-aware-comparison/summary.json), [all per-image rows](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-aware-comparison/per_image.csv), and [paired location summary](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-aware-paired-summary.json) preserve the complete results.

The tuning set has been inspected for earlier model comparisons and comes from NYU mirror training shards. The original checkpoint's pretraining images are not authenticated; it may already have seen some images or rooms. None of the measured tuning differences is an independent final-test result. We have also identified official evaluation scene names using a mapping supported by 31 of 32 exact checks, with one near-identical but non-exact case; remaining cases and physical location identities are not fully verified. The comparative experiment itself did not run the 654 final evaluation images.

This follow-up was chosen **after** inspecting the first comparison, and the tuning set was already inspected; it is not a blinded experiment. ## Desktop TensorRT accuracy and timing — 14 November

The fixed [TensorRT configuration](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_scene_tensorrt.json) selected seed 42 for the original and each of the four recovered pruning methods before the tuning comparison. Each was exported to ONNX, checked against CPU PyTorch on one **training** image, and used to build a desktop TensorRT 10.3 engine with FP16 tactics enabled. Some layers may still run in FP32. The first export script incorrectly labeled its supplied training image as a validation image in three parity reports; those outputs and logs remain, and corrected reports were generated separately. The corrected five ONNX checks had maximum absolute errors 2.38e-6 to 3.81e-6 on that one input; this is not full-set validation or evidence of Orin compatibility.

On all 445 tuning images, the matching desktop TensorRT/PyTorch mean-image RMSE pairs in native units were: original **0.282107 / 0.281928**, weight **0.285215 / 0.284867**, average-ablation **0.282520 / 0.282455**, scene-aware **0.281560 / 0.281534**, random **0.284596 / 0.284614**. Overall means stayed close on this dataset, but that can hide local errors: the maximum absolute output difference anywhere on the 445 images was **1.396948** for original, **0.850651** weight, **0.415020** average-ablation, **0.062409** scene-aware and **0.741937** random. These are output-to-output differences, not the depth prediction error against ground truth. We have not established why the outliers occur or accepted them for deployment.

As a diagnostic only, a 0.1-native-unit absolute output-difference cutoff flagged at least one pixel in 21 original, 19 weight, four average-ablation, zero scene-aware and 23 random images. For original, 1,100 of 22,328,320 output pixels exceeded this arbitrary cutoff, including a 1.396948 difference on `bathroom_0050/00076.h5`. This cutoff was chosen for inspection, not as a previously agreed deployment tolerance; lower maximum difference on one model/seed and this dataset is not a general precision-robustness claim. The full records and largest per-image differences are in [TensorRT tuning results](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-tensorrt-tuning-v2/summary.json), [per-image CSV](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-tensorrt-tuning-v2/per_image.csv), and [outlier summary](../../results/evaluation/2026-11-14-scene-tensorrt-outliers.json). The earlier evaluation output is preserved separately; v2 added per-image difference diagnostics.

After locating that largest original-model outlier, we built a separate desktop TensorRT engine **without enabling FP16 tactics** and evaluated the same post-selected image. Its maximum absolute difference from the same PyTorch checkpoint was **0.143082**, versus **1.396948** for the FP16-enabled engine. This suggests the runtime/precision configuration matters for that image, but it does not isolate a particular operation: TensorRT can select different implementations and its default TF32 setting was not explicitly controlled. The sample was chosen after looking at tuning outputs, so this is a diagnostic, not a representative accuracy test. See [diagnostic configuration](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_trt_outlier_diagnostic.json) and [build/measurement record](../../results/benchmarks/2026-11-14-scene-trt-fp32-outlier/summary.json).

For latency, 50 warm-ups and 300 synchronized model-only calls were repeated in forward then reverse order for each saved engine. The original medians were **0.388433 / 0.324522 ms**, and scene-aware **0.324578 / 0.316602 ms**. The substantial change in the original across the ordered passes, uncontrolled power/temperature, one engine build per model, and TensorRT warnings about synchronization on the default stream prevent a reliable speedup claim. The fixed replay settings, all methods and raw samples are in [timing configuration](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_scene_tensorrt_replay.json) and [timing results](../../results/benchmarks/2026-11-14-scene-trt-replay/summary.json). These are desktop numbers, exclude preprocessing and transfers, and cannot establish Orin or end-to-end performance.

**Professor question: Why can similar RMSE still have a large maximum runtime difference?** RMSE aggregates errors over many pixels and images; a small number of outlier predictions can be diluted. The maximum compares the two runtime outputs at one pixel and has different sensitivity. Both provide useful information, but neither alone establishes deployment safety or a cause. Follow up by locating those pixels and comparing the original and pruned models' precision-sensitive operations under matched desktop settings, then validate the chosen runtime on Orin.

## TensorRT checks for the deterministic checkpoints

The [follow-up TensorRT configuration](../../configs/experiment_2026-11-14_deterministic_tensorrt.json) fixed the original and scene-aware seed-42 checkpoints before inspecting deterministic tuning results. On all 445 tuning images, desktop TensorRT FP16-enabled mean-image RMSE was **0.282669 original / 0.283390 scene-aware**, compared with matching PyTorch **0.282433 / 0.283273**. At the exploratory 0.1-unit threshold, 27 original images and one scene-aware image had at least one flagged pixel; the threshold was not a predefined deployment limit. The maximum in both models occurred on `bathroom_0050/00076.h5`. These are one-seed desktop runtime observations, not proof that either model is generally safer or suitable for Orin. Forward/reverse order, a large change across the original's passes, uncontrolled clocks/thermals, only one build per model, and default-stream synchronization warnings prevent a reliable speedup claim.
