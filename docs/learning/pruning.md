# Structured pruning

**Status: two exploratory decoder candidates implemented; neither is a demonstrated Orin optimization.** A convolution has input and output channels. Structured channel pruning physically removes selected channels, their associated convolution filters and matching normalization parameters. Every downstream layer consuming those channels must have matching input channels removed. Depthwise convolutions require their group count to stay consistent with their input/output channels. Additive skip connections couple the channels on both branches: two tensors cannot be added unless their shapes match.

A pruning proposal is a concrete channel configuration, not merely zero-valued weights. The workflow is to map dependencies, select channels, create a structurally smaller model, transfer surviving weights, test tensor shapes and finite output, measure accuracy, then fine-tune on training data. Compare the same candidate before and after fine-tuning; include a fine-tuning-only control if claiming the change came from pruning rather than extra training.

NetAdapt-style selection estimates cost on the target platform and chooses a candidate under a latency constraint using tuning accuracy. Fewer parameters or MACs do not guarantee lower latency: kernel selection, memory traffic, tensor shapes and layer fusion matter. Desktop measurements can help debug proposals, but final Orin-specific selection requires actual Orin measurements. The proposed target is lower Orin FP16 latency than the reference pruned model with no more than 0.02 m RMSE increase, after depth units and evaluation compatibility are verified. No such improvement has been demonstrated yet.

## The actual candidate and why these channels were chosen

The recovered model has 16 channels after `decode_conv5.1` and before `decode_conv6.0`. This point occurs **after** the last additive skip, so removing channels there does not require slicing both branches of an addition. We implemented one fixed proposal in `src/fastdepth_project/pruning.py`: keep 12 channels with the largest absolute weights in the final 1×1 output convolution. Ties break by original index. This is a simple, cheap heuristic, not NetAdapt and not evidence that these are the least important channels; it ignores activation magnitudes, batch-normalization scaling and device latency. Four channels—1, 3, 7 and 10—were removed.

The output filters of `decode_conv5.1.0`, the corresponding batch-normalization weights, biases and running statistics, and input channels of `decode_conv6.0` are sliced with the same retained-index vector. Remaining tensors and skip paths are preserved. The result physically changes 16 channels to 12; it is not a zero mask. It has 1,360,550 parameters versus 1,360,786 in the starting model: 236 fewer, approximately 0.0173%. This tiny reduction is unlikely by itself to establish a useful speedup; actual timing is required. A keep-all test matched the original model exactly, and a 12-channel test checked tensor shapes, finite output and backward gradients.

## Predeclared tuning comparison

Before training, `configs/experiment_2026-11-13_lastdecoder12.json` fixed one candidate, one epoch, seed 42, Adam at 1e-5 and the two-scene split. On 16 **tuning** images, original model mean-image RMSE was 0.116838 before training and 0.400989 after matched fine-tuning. The pruned candidate was 0.295998 before and 0.271104 after fine-tuning. These native-unit results are from one tuning scene, not the 654-image final evaluation set. Fine-tuning improved this candidate's RMSE relative to its own starting point, but its result remained worse than the original unmodified model on this tiny tuning set. The original model's tuning RMSE worsened markedly after the same training budget. Do not claim the proposed improvement succeeded; the change in running batch-normalization statistics is one hypothesis to investigate, not a confirmed cause. Detailed raw records are in `results/training/2026-11-13-scene-{control,pruned}/`.

**Professor question: Why choose the last stage?** It avoids the coupled skip branches, allowing us to validate a safe first structural operation quickly. The trade-off is that only a very small fraction of total parameters is removed. **Why not pick more channels based on validation accuracy?** The 654-file evaluation is reserved as a final comparison; using it repeatedly for pruning selection would leak evaluation information. **Is this hardware-aware pruning?** No. This is a fixed weight-based proposal; real Orin latency measurements are still required to direct or validate a hardware-aware search.

## Repeated desktop timing of the structural candidate

Three alternating pairs of PyTorch FP32, batch-1, model-only runs used a preallocated zero input, 50 warm-ups and 500 synchronized calls each. Control run medians were 5.990, 6.223 and 6.179 ms; candidate medians were 6.609, 6.148 and 6.111 ms. The median of the three run medians was 6.179 ms for the control and 6.148 ms for the candidate. The candidate was slower in the first pair and slightly faster in the next two. Three non-randomized pairs with uncontrolled desktop power/thermals do not establish a reliable speedup, and these numbers say nothing about Orin or end-to-end timing. The raw per-call samples remain in the corresponding `results/benchmarks/2026-11-13-pruning-*` directories; the summary is `results/benchmarks/2026-11-13-pruning-timing-comparison.json`.

## Pruning across a depthwise layer

The first proposal saved very little, so a second fixed proposal reduces the first decoder stage from 200 to 160 internal channels. For channel j, we sum absolute weights in input channel j of the following stage's pointwise convolution and retain the 160 largest sums, breaking ties by original index. This simple score ignores activations, normalization and device latency; it is a hypothesis, not proof of channel importance.

The first pointwise filter has shape `[200, 512, 1, 1]`: selecting output dimension 0 leaves `[160, 512, 1, 1]`. Slice its following batch-normalization parameters and running statistics by the same indices. The next depthwise filter has one 5×5 filter per channel, `[200, 1, 5, 5]` with 200 groups; slicing to `[160, 1, 5, 5]` requires changing its input/output channel counts and group count to 160, and slicing its batch normalization too. The next pointwise filter changes from `[256, 200, 1, 1]` to `[256, 160, 1, 1]` by slicing its input dimension. Its 256 output channels remain unchanged, so the downstream additive skip still has compatible shapes. `prune_first_decoder` in `src/fastdepth_project/pruning.py` performs these coupled operations. A keep-all comparison was bit-exact; tests also check group counts, shape and gradients.

The corrected candidate has 1,328,906 parameters versus 1,360,786, a reduction of 31,880 (about 2.34%). On the same 16-image tuning scene, mean-image RMSE in native units was 0.146354 before and 0.170767 after one epoch with BN layers in evaluation mode. The matched original model was 0.116838 before and 0.122571 after training. Thus this proposal also fails to beat the unmodified original in this exploratory accuracy comparison; latency has not been measured for this candidate and the repeated tuning set cannot support a final claim. The configuration was recorded before this run, but the proposal and BN mode followed inspection of earlier tuning outcomes. See `configs/experiment_2026-11-13_firstdecoder160.json`, `checkpoints/candidates/2026-11-13-firstdecoder160-corrected/pruning.json` and `results/training/2026-11-13-firstdecoder160-frozenbn/summary.json`.

The first generated checkpoint had a misleading `lastdecoder` variant label due to a hard-coded metadata suffix. Its structural report and checkpoint were retained under `2026-11-13-firstdecoder160`; we corrected the label and generated a separate candidate before training. This was a naming error, not a measured accuracy failure.

**Why change groups?** A depthwise convolution assigns one group per channel; mismatching the group count and physical input channels makes the layer invalid. **Why retain 256 output channels?** The following skip addition requires matching channel shapes, so pruning only the preceding internal width avoids modifying both addition branches. **Does 2.34% fewer parameters mean 2.34% faster?** No: runtime depends on device kernels, tensor dimensions and memory behavior, and Orin latency has not been measured.

## Gradual pruning using task-loss ablation (14 November)

Rather than guessing importance from the size of a weight alone, `scripts/score_ablation.py` asks what happens to the depth loss when each channel is removed individually. For each of the 200 channels, it uses the unchanged original checkpoint and the same training images. Let `L0` be mean image-wise MAE on positive finite depth pixels and `Lj` be the same loss with channel j zeroed. The score is `Lj - L0`; low values indicate that removing this channel individually hurt the sampled training loss least. A negative score means an individual removal happened to reduce this training loss, not that it will generalize. Rank by ascending score, with channel index breaking ties. This is a computationally expensive, data-dependent heuristic, not a guarantee of optimal selection.

The mask is applied to the output of `decode_conv2[0]`, just before the next pointwise convolution. That location matters: zeroing before the depthwise convolution and its batch normalization can be undone by normalization offsets. A unit test compares a single masked channel with actual physical removal. To build the smaller model, `scripts/prune_candidate.py --stage first --ablation-report <scores.json> --keep N --output <new-directory>` verifies the source checkpoint hash, chooses the lowest scores, and uses the same coupled tensor slicing described above. A fixed-width sweep (192, 184 and 176 kept from 200) uses the same single-channel ranking and starts each candidate from the original model. This is gradual in width, **not iterative re-scoring**; channel interactions mean individual scores do not necessarily predict the effect of removing a group. `configs/experiment_2026-11-14_ablation.json` records the initial protocol.

The 32-image training baseline MAE was 0.196314, averaging each image equally. The eight lowest individual scores were all negative; removing one at a time lowered this particular training loss, which does not mean removing the eight together will improve tuning accuracy. The candidate widths 192, 184 and 176 contain 1,354,410, 1,348,034 and 1,341,658 parameters respectively, versus 1,360,786 in the original. They remove 6,376, 12,752 and 19,128 parameters. The expanded-subset recovery comparison is in the training note; all three remained less accurate on tuning than the original unmodified model. `results/training/2026-11-14-ablation-scores.json` records all scores and source hashes.

Only training samples contribute to scoring; separate tuning scenes are used to compare candidates, and the 654 evaluation samples are excluded from selection. A promising model still needs accuracy and repeated latency measurements, and selecting for Orin latency specifically requires the Orin. The actual measurements and any deviations from the initial protocol are recorded in the progress report.

### Desktop speed check

On 14 November, two PyTorch FP32 model-only timing passes on the RTX 3060 used untrained candidate checkpoints (the shapes are the same after fine-tuning), one forward and one reverse width order, a preallocated zero tensor, 50 warm-ups and 300 synchronized calls per run. Median latencies in milliseconds were: control **5.351 / 5.444**, 192 **5.361 / 5.535**, 184 **5.459 / 5.536**, and 176 **5.442 / 5.453**. None showed a convincing speedup against the control. These are small differences with only two runs and uncontrolled power/thermal state; the specific TensorRT FP16 deployment path and Orin were not measured. Raw samples are in `results/benchmarks/2026-11-14-ablation-*/latencies_ms.csv`, summary and hashes in `results/benchmarks/2026-11-14-ablation-comparison.json`, and the timing protocol was written after inspecting tuning outcomes in `configs/experiment_2026-11-14_ablation_timing.json`.

**Why ablate at the next pointwise input rather than multiply initial weights by zero?** It directly removes the channel's contribution after its depthwise convolution and batch normalization, which is equivalent to deleting the corresponding pointwise input in inference mode. **Why might a low score be misleading?** It measures each channel in isolation on a small nonrepresentative sample; multiple removed channels can interact, and scene-specific importance may differ. **Why distinguish masks from structural pruning?** Masks measure a hypothetical removal; the saved candidate physically changes tensor shapes and depthwise groups, enabling a runtime to use the smaller network.

### Matched weight-versus-ablation selection — 14 November

At the same 192-channel width, checkpoint, data and frozen-BN one-epoch recovery schedule, the weight-based candidate had tuning mean-image RMSE 0.440975 before and 0.448607 after training; the ablation candidate had 0.573695 before and 0.475736 after. The unmodified starting model was 0.428998, and its matched trained control 0.454392. Both candidates have 1,354,410 parameters but remove different channels. The weight-based method performed better in this one comparison; its trained score was better than the trained control, but worse than the original untrained control. The 64 tuning images come from two scenes, were repeatedly inspected, and no Orin latency was measured. Ranking generalization and the small difference from trained control remain unproven. See `configs/experiment_2026-11-14_simple_controls.json`, `results/training/2026-11-14-simple-controls.json`, and [four experiments](four_experiments.md).
